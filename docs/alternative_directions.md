# Where else this data can go

Written after the demand-based pricing tests came back mixed. The pipeline —
40.6m signed trades, validated surfaces, forward curves, 24/7 five-minute
returns, delta-hedged return machinery — is agnostic about the question. These
are the directions it supports, with feasibility probed rather than asserted.

---

## 1. The price of weekend risk in a market that never closes — RECOMMENDED

**The question.** French & Roll (1986) showed equity variance per calendar hour
collapses when the exchange is shut, and concluded that variance is generated by
trading. That test can never be clean in equities, because market closure and
the absence of trading are the same event. Crypto separates them: the market
never closes, but the traditional financial system does. So does variance still
fall at the weekend, and if so, is it priced?

**Fact 1 — it falls, sharply, in a market that is fully open.**

Annualized realized volatility from 5-minute returns:

| | Weekday | Weekend | Ratio | t |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| BTC (2,923 days) | 59.90% | 44.13% | **0.737** | 11.01 |
| ETH (1,711 days) | 79.02% | 68.29% | **0.864** | 3.06 |

BTC weekend volatility is **26% lower** despite continuous trading, with
Saturday the quietest day of the week (41.98%). Since crypto itself is open, the
weekend drop isolates information originating in traditional market hours —
macro releases, institutional flow, the ETF complex — from the mechanical effect
of an exchange being shut. That is a clean identification of a channel the
equity literature cannot separate.

**Fact 2 — the option market prices it, but only half of it.**

Deribit lists daily expiries on all seven weekdays, including Saturday and
Sunday. There is no equity analogue: you cannot buy an option expiring on a
Saturday when the exchange is closed. Near-ATM implied vol for options with
three days or less to expiry, by expiry weekday:

| Expiry day | BTC IV | ETH IV |
|---|---:|---:|
| Mon (spans the weekend) | 48.97 | 63.51 |
| Tue | 56.22 | 71.12 |
| Wed | 57.34 | 73.37 |
| Thu | 58.91 | 76.32 |
| Fri | 61.87 | 78.93 |
| Sat | 55.16 | 70.43 |
| **Sun** | **44.27** | **59.74** |

The pattern is coherent and the sign is right: contracts covering weekend
calendar time are cheaper, and Monday expiries — which span Saturday and Sunday
— are the second cheapest of the week. The market understands the effect exists.

But the magnitude is wrong for BTC:

| | Implied discount | Realized discount | Gap |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| BTC | **13.3%** | **26.3%** | **13.0 pts** |
| ETH | 11.0% | 13.6% | 2.6 pts |

BTC weekend options are priced as though the weekend were only half as quiet as
it actually is. ETH is close to fair. That asymmetry is itself a finding worth
explaining — BTC carries far more traditional-finance-linked flow, so it has more
weekend effect to price and apparently prices it less completely.

**Why it is a good paper.** The question is canonical (French–Roll), the
identification is genuinely novel and only available in crypto, the fact is
large and highly significant, the mispricing is tradeable, and the cross-asset
asymmetry gives a mechanism test. Everything needed is already built.

**Caveats before believing the magnitude.** The implied and realized comparisons
above are both unconditional. A real test must match observations by date and
condition on the level of volatility, since the weekday mix of high-vol regimes
is not uniform and short-dated IV carries a maturity-related bias. The
delta-hedged return machinery already in the repo gives the decisive version:
sell weekend-expiry straddles, hedge daily, and measure whether the premium is
actually earned. Do that before writing anything.

## 2. Who sells crypto tail risk, and are they paid?

Salvages the one unambiguous result from the demand-pressure work. End users
sell deep-OTM and buy at- and in-the-money, monotonically, in both currencies
across the full history — the mirror image of US index options. The natural
follow-up is who absorbs that tail exposure and what they earn for it, which the
existing delta-hedged return code answers directly by moneyness bucket.

Smaller in scope, but roughly 60% built already, and it does not depend on any
of the tests that failed.

## 3. What does signed order flow actually measure?

A methods paper around the open-interest reconciliation: the bound
|net signed flow| ≤ open interest holds (2.2% BTC, 3.6% ETH violations), but net
taker position is only a third to a half of open interest. Every paper in this
literature infers dealer inventory from signed flow and validates it only
indirectly, by showing the measure predicts what theory says it should. This is
a direct test, and it bounds what the inference can support.

Modest but safe, and the result is already in hand.

## Not recommended

**Liquidation cascades.** Forced flow is only 0.1–0.5% of trades in the modern
sample (it was 5.6% for ETH in 2019). Too thin to carry a paper.

**Crypto variance risk premium generally.** Crowded, and the marginal
contribution over existing work is unclear.
